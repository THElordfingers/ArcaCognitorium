The question is whether a **Lore Engine** is implementable — and what it 
would actually *be*.

Here's the honest assessment from The Builder:

---

**Yes. It is implementable. And it is needed.**

Right now, lore is *distributed and manual*. The `lore_prefix` in `app.py` is 
hardcoded. Entity `purpose` fields in the YAML files are hand-written. The 
trait-to-instruction map in `entity_compiler.py` produces generic behavioral 
instructions, not *voice*. There is no single source of truth for the register, 
the vocabulary, the cosmology, or the aesthetic rules of the Cogniverse. If you 
want to write new entity copy, a new boot message, a new status bar phrase, a 
new error — you are doing it from memory and instinct each time. That works 
when it's just you. It becomes fragile at scale and nearly impossible to share 
with other Wizards.

A **Lore Engine** would solve this by becoming the authoritative voice layer 
that everything else draws from.

---

**What it actually is:**

Not a separate running process. Not an LLM call on every message. It is a 
**structured data layer** — a set of authored documents and a compiler that 
translates them into usable artifacts — combined with an **in-session 
assistant** you can invoke to generate new lore-consistent copy.

**Three components:**

**1. The Lore Corpus** (`lore/` directory)
A collection of structured documents that define the Cogniverse's voice, 
cosmology, and vocabulary. Things like:
- `register.yaml` — tone rules, forbidden words (atelier), preferred 
constructions, ceremonial vs operational registers
- `cosmology.yaml` — the canonical facts of the tower: what it is, who the 
Wizard is, what the Council is, what memory layers mean in-world
- `vocabulary.yaml` — preferred terms (Arx Arcana, Grimoire, Filum, 
Chronicle, Tome), with forbidden alternatives
- `entity_voices/` — per-entity flavor notes: characteristic phrases, 
cadence, things each entity would *never* say

**2. The Lore Compiler** (`lore_compiler.py`)
At startup, reads the corpus and makes it available to the `EntityCompiler`. 
Instead of a hardcoded `lore_prefix` in `app.py`, the prefix is *rendered* from 
the cosmology. Entity instruction strings get a voice layer on top of the 
trait-behavioral layer. Boot messages, status phrases, error messages — all 
pulled from lore-consistent templates.

**3. The Lore Forge** (interactive tool)
An invocable mode — possibly `/lore` command or a dedicated Arx Arcana 
workshop — where you describe what you need ("write a dismissal message when 
the Socratic is sent away") and the system generates it using the corpus as a 
constraint document injected into the prompt. Output is lore-checked against 
the vocabulary list before being offered. You accept or regenerate. Accepted 
output can be written directly to the appropriate file.

---

**What this gives you practically:**

- Entity personalities stop being just trait sliders — they get *voice*. The 
Contrarian sounds like the Contrarian, not just "a high-challenge entity."
- When you add a new entity, new command, new page — you run it through the 
Forge and it comes out sounding native.
- Other Wizards installing the system get the same register. The Cogniverse has 
a consistent soul across instances.
- The `lore_prefix` that currently lives naked in `app.py` becomes a living 
document.

---

**What it is NOT:**

It does not replace your authorship. The corpus is written by you. The Forge 
assists but you accept. It is a **constraint system and an assistant**, not a 
generator running loose.

---

**Build order recommendation:**

This does not block v1 core feature work. It runs *parallel*. The immediate 
deliverable is the `lore/` corpus structure and the `register.yaml` — that 
document alone immediately gives every future piece of copy a written standard 
to be checked against. The compiler integration comes after v1 is proving 
itself in real use, when you know what phrases are actually appearing in the 
interface.

The Forge is a v1.2 feature. The corpus and vocabulary list can start now, 
today, as a document.
